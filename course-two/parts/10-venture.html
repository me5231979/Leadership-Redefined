Module A

How this story ends, and how it began

The idea

The ending is impressive. The beginning is the lesson. From 1998 to 2019, new commitments stayed roughly flat. Benchmarking for fiscal years 2017 to 2021 showed Vanderbilt raising $240 per living alumnus, against $1,200 to $3,000 at the leading peers. Total cash per living alumnus actually shrank over that span. Lutz’s summary: “We inherited a team that was underperforming in a variety of ways.”

An entrepreneurial leader does not start with a solution. They start with a clear-eyed diagnosis. Explore the one Lutz’s team made.

Select a quadrant

“Unlocking performance required that we drill into root causes.” Each quadrant shows what the team found, and what it meant.

Strengths

A strong brand. Pockets of talented people. Support from the Chancellor and the Board of Trust.

What it meant: the raw material was there. Leadership’s job was to connect it.

Weaknesses

A silo mentality. A rigid culture. A hostile environment. Out-of-date processes and systems.

What it meant: three of the four are about people and culture, not tools. The fix had to start there.

Opportunities

New leadership. An investment mentality. Willing fellow travelers.

What it meant: change needs allies. Find the people who already want it and give them a reason to lead.

Threats

Time not on our side. Pressure. Urgency within a fragile environment.

What it meant: move fast enough to show results, carefully enough not to break what was working.

Source: Vice Chancellor John Lutz, “Entrepreneurial Mindset: DAR Transformational Change,” presentation to Leadership Redefined, April 2026.

Module C

Know what kind of problem you have

This topic’s pre-work, “A Leader’s Framework for Decision Making,” makes one point every entrepreneur lives by: the right way to act depends on the kind of problem in front of you. Learn the four kinds, sort four situations, then check your own habits.

You’ll learnWhen to analyze, and when to run an experiment You’ll doSort four situations, rate eight habits Time7 min
The idea

Simple problems have a known best practice: sense, categorize, respond. Complicated problems have a right answer that takes expertise to find: sense, analyze, respond. Complex problems have no right answer up front; cause and effect only show up in hindsight, so you probe, sense, respond: run small, safe experiments and amplify what works. Chaotic situations need action first to restore order: act, sense, respond.

Most capstone challenges are complex. That is why the strongest pods propose a pilot before a full rollout. “Pilot, learn, scale” was also one of the Chancellor’s four margin levers last week.

David J. Snowden and Mary E. Boone, “A Leader’s Framework for Decision Making,” Harvard Business Review, November 2007.
