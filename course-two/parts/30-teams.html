Her path

Candice Storey Lee came to Vanderbilt from Madison, Alabama, as a student-athlete and became captain of the women’s basketball team. She earned three Vanderbilt degrees: a BS in human and organizational development (2000), an MEd (2002), and an EdD in higher education leadership and policy (2012). She rose from an internship to academic adviser, compliance, senior woman administrator, and deputy athletic director, and was named Vice Chancellor for Athletics and University Affairs and Athletic Director in May 2020, in the middle of a pandemic. She was Vanderbilt’s first woman athletic director and the first Black woman to lead an athletics department in the SEC.

Asked how she got there, she told the Vanderbilt Hustler: “I just said yes and I feel like I’ve been saying yes ever since.”

Module C

The learning zone, and three calls

Lee’s pairing of growth and accountability has a name in the research. Explore Amy Edmondson’s four zones, then make three calls drawn from real moments in Vanderbilt athletics.

You’ll learnWhy safety and standards need each other You’ll doExplore four zones, make three calls Time8 min

Select a zone

Psychological safety is the shared belief that it is safe to speak up, ask, and admit mistakes. Standards are how much the team expects of itself.

Apathy zone

Low safety, low standards. People keep their heads down and do the minimum.

Sounds like: “Not my job.”

Comfort zone

High safety, low standards. People like each other, and no one is stretched.

Sounds like: “We had a great meeting,” with nothing decided.

Anxiety zone

High standards, low safety. People work hard and hide problems, so mistakes surface late.

Sounds like: silence when the leader asks, “Any concerns?”

Learning zone

High standards and high safety. People take on hard goals and say what is not working. This is where high-performing teams live.

Sounds like: “I missed that. Here is what I learned and what I will do next.” Lee’s “growth and accountability” in one sentence.

Source: Amy C. Edmondson, The Fearless Organization, Wiley, 2019; and “Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams,” Administrative Science Quarterly, 1999.

Scenario 01 · The Monday after

Your team just had its biggest win ever

A project your team delivered is being praised all the way to the Chancellor’s office. It is Monday morning and the team is still celebrating.

Our take: after the Alabama win, Lee called it “just the beginning.” Recognize the people, capture what worked, and point the energy at the next goal.

Scenario 02 · The star’s bad moment

Your best performer crosses a line in public

A high performer on your team posts something unprofessional about a partner office. Colleagues across campus have seen it.

Our take: when Vanderbilt’s star quarterback posted a profane message in December 2025, Lee publicly called it unacceptable and, in the same statement, affirmed support as he learned from it. Clear standard, continued support. Work with HR on anything that rises to a conduct issue.

Scenario 03 · The slow start

A new leader’s first year is rough

You hired a manager with the right values and a strong plan. Twelve months in, results are below target and people are asking whether you chose wrong.

Our take: Lee backed Clark Lea through a 2-10 first full season. She watched the culture and preparation, not only the scoreboard, and the results caught up. Patience works when it comes with clear milestones.
