    Module 4: Communication, Storytelling & Brand · Brand and reputation
    1

    Brand and reputation: close the gap.

    Brand is what you say about yourself. Reputation is what others say about you. They are separate but related, and the goal is to close the gap to achieve successful business results.

    Where are we today?
    1. Limited brand saliency. Strong awareness in the South; less recognition nationally.
    2. Lack of differentiation. For many, Vanderbilt lacks distinction from peers, which makes it harder to turn awareness into consideration.
    3. Universities under fire. Affordability, the admissions process, political ideologies, and increasing polarization.
    Module 4: Communication, Storytelling & Brand · Three flywheels

    Differentiation by design.

    Vanderbilt is a fighter brand: not the loudest, but deeply valued once discovered. High affinity, low saliency.

    Differentiation as a design principle
    1. Three reputation flywheels set Vanderbilt apart: execution with ambition, values-driven leadership, and athletics momentum.
    2. Each one turns on two kinds of assets: brand assets, what Vanderbilt says, and reputation assets, what others say.

    Module 4: Communication, Storytelling & Brand · The ground game

    Make the message spread.

    Reputation creates resources when air cover is carried through the ground game. The ground game then compounds reputation.

    The idea
    1. Air cover gets the story out. The ground game carries it to the people who matter.
    2. Heard is not enough. The goal is not simply to be heard. The goal is for the message to spread.
    3. You are the ground game. Leaders share assets directly and equip others to pass them on.

    How this story ends.

    Years of mediocre performance, then records. Guess first.

    What drove it

    The team named root causes, then changed people, process, and technology together. On three-year rolling averages, 2020 to 2025, new commitments grew from $149M to $314M while the investment grew from $28M to $44M. Results grew faster than spending, and the team kept an eye on efficiency.

    Early wins built momentum: events and hospitality, CRM and data, bringing the campaign to life, additional funding.

    Module 5: Entrepreneurial Mindset · The change moves

    Change people, process, and technology at once.

    Successful change leadership requires ambidexterity: alternating between assessment and action.

    The idea
    1. Assess: listen carefully to key constituents; make clear-eyed assessments.
    2. Act: deal with resistance; drive people, process, and technology together.
    3. Look beyond best practices for new ideas, even outside higher education.

    Four categories held every initiative: people and culture, collaboration and engagement, motivation and incentives, process and systems.

    Module 5: Entrepreneurial Mindset · Radical collaboration

    Nobody wins this alone.

    The Chancellor’s call for Radical Collaboration underpinned every change initiative in the turnaround.

    The idea

    The objective: maximize total philanthropic support for Vanderbilt to advance its mission.

    1. Donor-centric: work with the people you serve, rather than steering them.
    2. A team sport: multiple roles are needed; every Vanderbilt leader is a fundraiser.
    3. One Vanderbilt: cross-Vanderbilt strategies, supported by shared credit.

    Lead the people around it. Buy time with critics, send serious signals, and build trust with top producers.

    Module 5: Entrepreneurial Mindset · Your pilot

    Reframe it, then start small.

    Question the status quo, take a calculated risk, and let an early win build momentum.

    The idea
    1. Question the assumption. Flip it with “what if.”
    2. Look beyond best practices, including outside higher education.
    3. Pilot, validate, then scale. Agree first on the result that earns scale.

    Example. A past pod asked: what if attending campus events seeded a student’s investment account? Their plan piloted first, then went campus-wide.

    Your brief · Strong metrics

    Would you know in 90 days if it worked?

    A strong metric names what changes, by how much, for whom, and by when.

    Anatomy of a metric
    1. Baseline: where it is today. Without it, no one can tell if you improved.
    2. Target: the number you are aiming for.
    3. Date: when you will check.
    4. Outcome, not output: measure what changes for people, like days saved or students returning, not what you produced, like emails sent or a site launched.
