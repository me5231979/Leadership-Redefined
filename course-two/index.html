    The belief

    Great ideas need builders, guardians, and teams. Leadership Redefined exists so that the leaders who run Vanderbilt every day can start bold work, protect the trust it depends on, and build teams that keep winning.

    The institutional why · Crescere aude

    Vanderbilt will define the great university of the 21st Century and be it.

    That is Chancellor Diermeier’s institutional vision, one sentence with no hedge in it. The how is equally blunt: operate at uncommon speed, agility, and scale to address society’s challenges through education and research, and help reclaim the essential role of leading research universities in driving American prosperity. Our motto has always been the dare, Crescere aude, dare to grow.

    Areas of Focus

    Three ways Vanderbilt delivers on the vision

    1

    Exceptional Core Operations

    With speed, agility and scale, Vanderbilt will distinguish itself with innovative and impactful capabilities to ensure that we have the talent, resources and reputation necessary to deliver on our vision.

    2

    Bold Strategic Initiatives

    With “exceptional core operations” as our fuel, Vanderbilt will pursue increasingly bold initiatives that enable and enhance the reach and impact of our education and research mission.

    3

    Values Leadership

    Through strategic advocacy efforts and modeling the role of an essential research university, Vanderbilt will stimulate industry change and combat existential threats to our education and research mission.

    How this course delivers

    These three areas are also your capstone’s three focus areas. This week you learn to design a solution the way an entrepreneur would, small enough to start and built to scale; to protect the reputation every bold idea borrows against; and to lead the team that delivers it. That is how a pod’s brief becomes something the university can actually run.

    The reputation flywheel

    Reputation and resources feed each other in a loop, reputation attracts talent, funding, partnerships, and access, which build the reputation. The university spins that flywheel with execution with ambition, values-driven leadership, and athletics momentum. Here’s your place in it: a pilot launched in weeks instead of years is execution with ambition; a hard call made in the open is values-driven leadership; and the athletics momentum you will study in section 03 is a lesson in how high-performing teams turn effort into results. Every promise your team keeps feeds the wheel from your desk.

    Module A

    How this story ends, and how it began

    Vice Chancellor John Lutz taught entrepreneurial mindset to this program with a case, not a definition: the transformation of Vanderbilt’s fundraising team. He started with the ending.

    You’ll learnWhat changed, in numbers You’ll doRead the case, explore the diagnosis Time6 min
    $345MNew commitments in 2025, another record-breaking yearUp from $63M in 1998.
    $198MCash raised in 2025The best cash year since the VUMC split.
    +22%Compound annual growth in new commitments, 2020 to 2025Cash grew 10% a year over the same span.
    +111%Growth in new commitments while fundraising investment grew 57%Three-year rolling averages, 2020 to 2025: $149M to $314M raised, $28M to $44M invested.
    The idea

    The ending is impressive. The beginning is the lesson. From 1998 to 2019, new commitments stayed roughly flat. Benchmarking for fiscal years 2017 to 2021 showed Vanderbilt raising $240 per living alumnus, against $1,200 to $3,000 at the leading peers. Total cash per living alumnus actually shrank over that span. Lutz’s summary: “We inherited a team that was underperforming in a variety of ways.”

    An entrepreneurial leader does not start with a solution. They start with a clear-eyed diagnosis. Explore the one Lutz’s team made.

    Select a quadrant

    “Unlocking performance required that we drill into root causes.” Each quadrant shows what the team found, and what it meant.

    Strengths

    A strong brand. Pockets of talented people. Support from the Chancellor and the Board of Trust.

    What it meant: the raw material was there. Leadership’s job was to connect it.

    Weaknesses

    A silo mentality. A rigid culture. A hostile environment. Out-of-date processes and systems.

    What it meant: three of the four are about people and culture, not tools. The fix had to start there.

    Opportunities

    New leadership. An investment mentality. Willing fellow travelers.

    What it meant: change needs allies. Find the people who already want it and give them a reason to lead.

    Threats

    Time not on our side. Pressure. Urgency within a fragile environment.

    What it meant: move fast enough to show results, carefully enough not to break what was working.

    Source: Vice Chancellor John Lutz, “Entrepreneurial Mindset: DAR Transformational Change,” presentation to Leadership Redefined, April 2026.

    Module C

    Know what kind of problem you have

    This topic’s pre-work, “A Leader’s Framework for Decision Making,” makes one point every entrepreneur lives by: the right way to act depends on the kind of problem in front of you. Learn the four kinds, sort four situations, then check your own habits.

    You’ll learnWhen to analyze, and when to run an experiment You’ll doSort four situations, rate eight habits Time7 min
    The idea

    Simple problems have a known best practice: sense, categorize, respond. Complicated problems have a right answer that takes expertise to find: sense, analyze, respond. Complex problems have no right answer up front; cause and effect only show up in hindsight, so you probe, sense, respond: run small, safe experiments and amplify what works. Chaotic situations need action first to restore order: act, sense, respond.

    Most capstone challenges are complex. That is why the strongest pods propose a pilot before a full rollout. “Pilot, learn, scale” was also one of the Chancellor’s four margin levers last week.

    David J. Snowden and Mary E. Boone, “A Leader’s Framework for Decision Making,” Harvard Business Review, November 2007.

    The reputation feedback loop

    Chancellor Diermeier wrote the book this topic’s pre-work comes from, Reputation Rules. In his strategy presentation to the program he drew the idea as a loop.

    You’ll learnWhy reputation is infrastructure, not a campaign You’ll doRead the loop, flip four cards Time5 min
    The idea

    A strong reputation is what lets Vanderbilt recruit a star researcher, close a major gift, win a partner, or get a call returned in Washington. Those resources then produce results that build more reputation. The loop runs in reverse too: one badly handled moment can drain resources that took years to build. High-priority media mentions grew from roughly 50,000 to 70,000 a year before 2020 to 322,296 in fiscal year 2026 to date (as of January 2026). More visibility means more upside, and more exposure.

    Module B

    How trust is earned

    In Reputation Rules, Diermeier argues that when something goes wrong, stakeholders judge an institution less on the facts than on whether they trust it. He describes four drivers of that trust. Explore each one.

    You’ll learnThe four drivers of trust You’ll doExplore a four-part model Time4 min

    Select a driver

    Strong reputations are built on all four. A crisis usually exposes the weakest one.

    Transparency

    Being open about what happened and what you are doing about it. Silence gets read as hiding.

    From your seat: explain the trade-off behind a hard decision, and say what you do not yet know.

    Expertise

    Showing you know how to fix the problem and have the competence to do it.

    From your seat: bring the facts and the plan, not just the apology.

    Commitment

    Showing that the issue matters to you and that you will see it through, with visible action and follow-up.

    From your seat: name an owner and a date, then report back when you said you would.

    Empathy

    Showing you understand how the people affected feel. Facts without empathy land as cold.

    From your seat: start with the person who was affected before you start with the process.

    Source: Daniel Diermeier, Reputation Rules: Strategies for Building Your Company’s Most Valuable Asset, McGraw-Hill, 2011.

    What you can do, starting tomorrow

Vanderbilt’s commitment to open forums, institutional neutrality, and civil discourse gives you the footing.

    Module C

    The learning zone, and three calls

    Lee’s pairing of growth and accountability has a name in the research. Explore Amy Edmondson’s four zones, then make three calls drawn from real moments in Vanderbilt athletics.

    You’ll learnWhy safety and standards need each other You’ll doExplore four zones, make three calls Time8 min

    Select a zone

    Psychological safety is the shared belief that it is safe to speak up, ask, and admit mistakes. Standards are how much the team expects of itself.

    Apathy zone

    Low safety, low standards. People keep their heads down and do the minimum.

    Sounds like: “Not my job.”

    Comfort zone

    High safety, low standards. People like each other, and no one is stretched.

    Sounds like: “We had a great meeting,” with nothing decided.

    Anxiety zone

    High standards, low safety. People work hard and hide problems, so mistakes surface late.

    Sounds like: silence when the leader asks, “Any concerns?”

    Learning zone

    High standards and high safety. People take on hard goals and say what is not working. This is where high-performing teams live.

    Sounds like: “I missed that. Here is what I learned and what I will do next.” Lee’s “growth and accountability” in one sentence.

    Source: Amy C. Edmondson, The Fearless Organization, Wiley, 2019; and “Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams,” Administrative Science Quarterly, 1999.

    6/8“You can’t always control for winning, but you can always control your ability to compete.” What does it ask of a team leader?

    Control the controllables. Hackman’s research points the same way: leaders shape the conditions.

    7/8High standards with low psychological safety put a team in which zone?

    Anxiety. People work hard but hide problems. Add safety and you reach the learning zone (Edmondson, 2019).

    8/8Which is a strong success metric for a capstone brief?

    Baseline, target, date, and an outcome people feel.

    Glossary

    Ambidexterity:
    alternating between assessment and action while leading change.
    Benchmarking:
    comparing your results with peers, ideally the best ones.
    CAGR:
    compound annual growth rate; the average yearly growth over a period.
    Complex problem:
    one with no known answer up front, where cause and effect show up only in hindsight.
    CRM:
    customer relationship management system; software that tracks relationships, such as with donors.
    Dependability:
    team members trusting each other to deliver quality work on time.
    Kaizen:
    a practice of small, continuous improvements, borrowed from manufacturing.
    Learning zone:
    high standards together with high psychological safety.
    Pilot:
    a small, time-limited test of an idea before a full rollout.
    Psychological safety:
    a shared belief that it is safe to speak up, ask, and admit mistakes.
    Reframe:
    stating a problem in a new way that opens different solutions.
    Reputation feedback loop:
    reputation attracts resources, and resources build reputation.
    Reputational statement:
    one or two sentences on why a project matters for Vanderbilt’s credibility and visibility.
    Revenue sharing:
    the college sports model under which schools share revenue directly with student-athletes.
    SWOT:
    strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats.
    Team charter:
    a short agreement on purpose, owners, decisions, and accountability.
