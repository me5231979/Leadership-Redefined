    Module 4 · Entrepreneurial mindset
    1

    Start with the Chancellor on the entrepreneurial mindset.

    What it means

    An entrepreneurial mindset means acting like a founder inside an institution: spot a problem or opening, diagnose it honestly, act before everything is certain, test small, learn fast, and scale what works with the resources you can reach. It is how a large university moves at uncommon speed, agility, and scale.

    John M. Lutz, Vice Chancellor for Development and Alumni Relations, taught it through a case: Vanderbilt’s fundraising turnaround. It began with a diagnosis, not a solution.

    Module 4 · The turnaround

    How this story ends.

    Twenty years of flat fundraising, then a record. Guess first.

    Tap a tile, make your guess

    What drove it

    From 1998 to 2019, new commitments stayed roughly flat. The team benchmarked against the best, named root causes (three of four weaknesses were about people and culture), and changed people, process, and technology together. Over 2020 to 2025, on three-year rolling averages, new commitments grew from $149M to $314M while the fundraising investment grew from $28M to $44M. Results more than doubled; spending rose about half. That is the entrepreneurial point: invest where the diagnosis points, and measure the return.

    Module 4 · The change moves

    Change people, process, and technology at once.

    Every tactic had an owner, a date, and one of four homes.

    The idea

    Ambidexterity: alternate between assessment and action. Lutz named five moves.

    1. Listen carefully to key constituents, which also buys time with critics.
    2. Make clear-eyed assessments. Benchmark against the best, not the average.
    3. Deal with resistance and underperformance while giving hope to future leaders.
    4. Drive people, process, and technology agendas at the same time.
    5. Look beyond best practices for new ideas, including outside higher education.

    Example. The four homes for every tactic: People and Culture, Collaboration and Engagement, Motivation and Incentives, Process and Systems.

    Module 4 · Radical collaboration

    Nobody wins this alone.

    The Chancellor’s call for Radical Collaboration underpinned every change in the turnaround. Your pod’s idea will need the same.

    The idea
    1. Start from the people you serve. Gift proposals reflected donor interests, aligned with the mission. Priorities were served by working with donors, not by steering them.
    2. It is a team sport. Many roles are needed to execute a strategy, and every Vanderbilt leader is a fundraiser.
    3. Operate as One Vanderbilt. Donors who support several areas give the most, so every key prospect had a cross-Vanderbilt strategy, backed by shared credit.

    Lead the people around the change. Lutz kept every key constituent connected: buy time with potential critics; send serious signals that you mean business; repair an image as a poor partner; build trust and loyalty with top producers; and bring distinctive value and an outside view. Early wins built the momentum for everything that followed.

    Example. The team rebuilt relationships with deans and created shared credit for cross-school gifts, so two schools could win the same gift instead of competing for it.

    Module 4 · Your pilot

    Reframe it, then start small.

    One past pod asked: what if attending a career workshop seeded a student’s investment account?

    How to reframe
    1. Name the assumption everyone makes about the problem.
    2. Flip it with “what if.” Most new ideas start as a reversed assumption.
    3. Borrow from outside higher education: a hotel, a hospital, a retailer, a bank.
    4. Shrink it to a pilot small enough to start without new approvals.
    5. Set a scale rule before you start, so the result decides, not opinions.

    Builds your brief Your pilot starts section 5, and your scale rule seeds section 6. Both are added to your draft automatically.

    Capture your pod’s reframe and pilot

    See a strong example

    THE USUAL ASSUMPTION: Students will come to career and financial-literacy programs if the programs are good. THE REFRAME: What if attending a program put real money into a student's own investment account? BORROWED FROM OUTSIDE HIGHER ED: Employer savings matches, and a brokerage partnership another university already runs. THE PILOT: One semester, a handful of departments, $10 to $25 per event, paid from event budgets they already have. WE SCALE IF: Attendance rises and students come back for a second event. (Adapted from a past Leadership Redefined pod.)

    Module 4 · What this means for me

    What this means for me.

    Entrepreneurial mindset, applied twice: to your capstone, and to the team you lead.

    For your capstone
    1. Diagnose before you solve: name root causes, and how many are about people.
    2. Design the smallest pilot that proves your idea, with a scale rule set in advance.
    3. Map the people: partners who share credit, a likely critic, top producers, and your early win.
    As a manager
    1. Alternate assessment and action. Listen and benchmark, then move, then look again.
    2. Change people, process, and technology together. Fixing one alone rarely sticks.
    3. Run small experiments on complex problems, and celebrate early wins out loud.

    Module 4 · Go deeper: kinds of problems

    Analyze it, or test it?

    The right move depends on the kind of problem. Most capstones are complex: run a pilot.

    Go deeper · a new idea

    Why it is worth knowing. Not every problem should be solved the same way. Snowden and Boone’s Cynefin framework sorts problems into simple, complicated, complex, and chaotic, and shows why complex problems call for small experiments instead of long analysis.

    How it complements this lesson. Vice Chancellor Lutz’s turnaround alternated assessment and action, and the Chancellor’s levers end with “pilot, learn, scale.” Cynefin explains when piloting beats planning. O’Reilly and Tushman’s research on ambidexterity shows how organizations run today’s work while building tomorrow’s.

    • A Leader’s Framework for Decision MakingDavid J. Snowden and Mary E. Boone, Harvard Business Review, November 2007
    • The Ambidextrous OrganizationCharles A. O’Reilly III and Michael L. Tushman, Harvard Business Review, April 2004
    The four kinds of problem
    1. Simple: a known best practice exists. Sense, categorize, respond.
    2. Complicated: a right answer exists, and experts can find it. Sense, analyze, respond.
    3. Complex: no right answer up front; cause and effect show up only in hindsight. Probe with small, safe experiments, sense what happens, respond by growing what works.
    4. Chaotic: no time to analyze. Act to restore order, then sense and respond.

    Example. Snowden and Boone, A Leader’s Framework for Decision Making, HBR, 2007. The mistake to avoid: treating a complex problem like a complicated one, and spending months analyzing what only a pilot can reveal.

    Module 5 · Reputational stewardship
    1

    Start with the Chancellor on reputation.

    Reputation brings talent and funding. Trust is what protects it when something goes wrong.

    The idea

    Reputation is infrastructure. In Reputation Rules, Chancellor Daniel Diermeier argues that in a crisis people judge you less on the facts than on whether they trust you.

    1. A system, not a campaign: built every day by service, operations, and decisions.
    2. A source of options: trusted institutions get the benefit of the doubt and a seat at the table.
    3. A platform for momentum: each win is easier to build on.
    4. A converter: it turns visibility into resources, but only when someone carries the story.

    The trust radar. Picture trust as a shape on four axes: transparency, expertise, commitment, and empathy. Strong on all four is a full diamond. A crisis finds the dent. Two more tools from the Chancellor: credibility transfer (a trusted third party, such as an outside expert or a partner, lends you their credibility) and room to maneuver (trust built before a crisis buys time and options during it).

    At Vanderbilt. The Chancellor’s team has applied these tools in real moments, from campus protests to public scrutiny of clinical care.

    Module 5 · Where reputation is made

    Your audiences hear you through others.

    Most people meet Vanderbilt through the media, and the media is pushed by a second circle of activists. Know where a story sits, and where it is heading.

    The idea
    1. What shapes reputation. The university speaks to its audiences directly, but most of what they hear arrives through the media. Around the media sits a second circle: activists and advocates who decide what the media and your constituents pay attention to.
    2. The terrain. Coverage sits on two axes: how important the issue is to society, and how interested a mass audience is. Simple reporting (low, low) is the wire brief. In-depth coverage (important, niche) runs on experts and data. Extensive reporting (popular, light) is cable infotainment. Mass-market coverage (both high) runs on testimony and drama.
    3. Stories migrate. A story rarely stays where it starts. When it moves up and to the right, the rules change: data stops being enough, and a human voice and empathy matter most.

    The Chancellor’s examples. A routine sports story became a story about the rise of antisemitism after a post about Kanye West. Clinical care guidelines became a “harming children” story through a social media campaign and political controversy about transgender issues. Both moved from a quiet corner of the map to the loudest one.

    Module 5 · Act while you have control

    The best time to act is before it feels urgent.

    As an issue grows, your control falls and the stakes rise. Most leaders act when the lines cross. By then the story is no longer yours.

    The idea
    1. Reputation dynamics. Early on, control is high and stakes are low: that is the optimal time to act. The typical time to act is later, when stakes spike and control has already slipped away.
    2. Before: prevention (fix what could go wrong) and preparation (who speaks, what we would say, who we call).
    3. During: identification (spot it early), containment (stop the spread, tell the people affected), and solution (fix the cause, with an owner and a date).
    4. After: learning, which feeds the next round of prevention and preparation. The cycle never ends: always learning.

    Example. A complaint about a confusing form gets ten replies online on Monday. Fixing the form and replying that afternoon is the optimal time. Waiting for a reporter’s call on Thursday is the typical time.

    1. Make your pod a team: clear roles, an owner and a date for every step.

    Module 6 · Go deeper: the learning zone

    High standards need a safe room.

    Safety without standards is comfort. Standards without safety is anxiety.

    Go deeper · a new idea

    Why it is worth knowing. Harvard’s Amy Edmondson found that teams learn and perform best when high standards meet psychological safety: the shared belief that it is safe to speak up, ask, and admit mistakes.

    How it complements this lesson. Lee’s principles pair high expectations with accountability for how people respond. Edmondson’s research shows the climate that makes that possible, and J. Richard Hackman’s shows that leaders shape performance most through the conditions they create, the same point Lee makes about controlling the controllables.

    • How to turn a group of strangers into a teamAmy C. Edmondson, TED, 2017
    • Why Teams Don’t WorkInterview with J. Richard Hackman, Harvard Business Review, May 2009
    How leaders build it

    Psychological safety is a shared belief that it is safe to speak up, ask, and admit mistakes. Amy Edmondson’s research shows leaders build it on purpose:

    1. Frame the work as learning. Say out loud that the task is new and you need everyone’s eyes.
    2. Admit your own limits. “I may be missing something” gives others permission to speak.
    3. Ask real questions, and invite the quietest person first.
    4. Respond well to bad news. Thank the messenger, then fix the problem. How you react the first time decides whether you hear the second.

    Go deeper · a new idea

    Why it is worth knowing. Google studied more than 180 of its own teams in Project Aristotle and found that how a team works matters more than who is on it. Psychological safety mattered most.

    How it complements this lesson. Your pod is a team with a deadline. This check turns Lee’s principles and Edmondson’s research into five questions you can ask your pod this week.

    • Guide: Understand team effectivenessGoogle re:Work, Project Aristotle
    What the research found

    Google studied more than 180 of its own teams in Project Aristotle and found that who is on a team matters less than how the team works. Five dynamics stood out: psychological safety (it is safe to take risks with each other), dependability (people deliver quality work on time), structure and clarity (clear goals, roles, and plans), meaning (the work matters to each person), and impact (people believe the work makes a difference). Safety mattered most.

    Your brief · Strong metrics

    Would you know in 90 days if it worked?

    This last part finishes your pod’s capstone brief. Start with the section most briefs get wrong: a strong metric names what changes, by how much, for whom, and by when.

    Anatomy of a metric
    1. Baseline: where it is today. Without it, no one can tell if you improved.
    2. Target: the number you are aiming for.
    3. Date: when you will check.
    4. Outcome, not output: measure what changes for people, like days saved or students returning, not what you produced, like emails sent or a site launched.

    Your brief · Carry it forward

    Live it, then teach it.

    The program ends in November. What you learned has to outlast it: use it where people can see, and pass it to the people you lead.

    The idea
    1. Embody it. Use the tools in the open, so your team learns them by watching. Ask “which area of focus does this serve?” in a planning meeting. Check the trust radar before a hard message. Say “this is complex, so let’s pilot it.”
    2. Share it. Teach one idea to your team within two weeks. Explaining an idea to others is one of the most reliable ways to keep it yourself; researchers call it the protégé effect.
    3. Multiply it. The vision needs more leaders who think this way. Name the next person who should be in this room.

    Example. A director spends ten minutes of a team meeting on the reputation engine, then asks each person which resource their own project grows: talent, funding, partnerships, or access.
