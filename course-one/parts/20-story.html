Module A

Brand and reputation: close the gap

Vice Chancellor for Communications and Marketing Steve Ertel opens with one distinction. Learn it, then sort six statements into the right circle.

You’ll learnWhy brand and reputation are separate but related You’ll doSort six statements Time5 min
The idea

Vanderbilt’s own read of its brand is candid. It is strong in the South but less known nationally. Many people see little to distinguish it from peers. And every university is fighting a wider devaluation of higher education. Brands come to exist in one of two ways, by default or by design: “We can let the world tell our story, or we can tell our own story.”

Ertel calls Vanderbilt a fighter brand: not the loudest, deeply valued once discovered, high affinity and low saliency. People who know Vanderbilt love it. Not enough people know it yet.

How does a fighter brand earn attention?

Module B

A spike without a system is noise

A big moment, like an upset win or a major gift, creates a spike in attention. It fades unless a steady drumbeat of stories carries it. Read the model, then explore the four things a fighter brand must do.

You’ll learnHow attention becomes trust, and trust becomes resources You’ll doExplore four moves Time5 min

Select a move

Each one comes from Vice Chancellor Ertel’s “The fighter brand must” slide. The panel shows what it looks like from a staff leader’s seat.

Earn attention thoughtfully

A fighter brand cannot outshout bigger names, so it chooses where to show up. From your seat: pick the two or three audiences your work matters to most and reach them well, instead of everyone a little.

Interrupt expectation strategically

People expect a university to be slow and cautious. Surprise them. From your seat: the three-day answer when they expected three weeks is a story people repeat.

Design moments bold enough to invite discovery

Spike moments give people a reason to look closer. Chancellor Diermeier puts it plainly: “A little controversy is good for us.” From your seat: treat a difficult topic as a chance to engage, not a thing to avoid.

Sustain meaning over time

Reputation works as infrastructure: a system, not a campaign; a source of options; a platform for momentum; a converter of visibility into resources. From your seat: keep telling the same true story long after the launch.

Air cover and ground game

Big campaigns and national stories are air cover. They create reputation assets: stories, speeches, earned media, paid media, social media. Reputation turns into resources only when the ground game carries those assets to priority stakeholders, such as government, donors, business, and prospective parents, and when those people pass them on peer to peer. In Ertel’s words, trust travels through proximity. That makes every staff leader part of the ground game.

Now the craft: how do great communicators structure a story?

Module C

The shape of a story that moves people

The program’s pre-work for this topic is Nancy Duarte’s talk on the structure shared by the greatest communicators. If you skipped it, the 18-minute video is in Resources. Here is the shape in one picture.

You’ll learnThe “what is, what could be” structure Time3 min, plus the optional talk
The idea

Great talks keep moving between what is (the frustrating present) and what could be (the better future). The gap between them creates the tension that makes people lean in. The story ends with a call to action and a picture of the new bliss: how the world looks once the audience acts. For a capstone brief, the “what is” is your challenge, the “what could be” is your solution, and the call to action is what you need the judges, and later a sponsor, to say yes to.

One more thing from past cohorts: the strongest pods opened with one real person. A pod proposing tuition support for staff began with a colleague’s own story of how a Vanderbilt degree “shaped my connection to this university.” A name and a moment beat a percentage every time.

Myth

Data persuades people who already care. A story makes them care. Lead with the person, then show the number that proves it is not one person.

Fact

The truth stays the same; the “what could be” changes. A dean wants research time back, a CFO wants the cost, a student wants the experience. Name your audiences before you write.

Myth

Communications builds the air cover. Trust travels through proximity, so leaders who understand the brand position, share reputation assets, and equip others to tell the story are the ground game.
